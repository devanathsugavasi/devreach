# Community Guide

General norms for common developer venues. **Rules change and every sub-community sets its own.** Use this as a starting point and always tell the user to read the current rules before posting.

## Hacker News — Show HN

- For things people can try right now: a running demo, a repo with install steps, a downloadable app. Not for landing pages, waitlists, or blog posts.
- Title format: `Show HN: <Name> – <what it does in plain words>`. No hype words, no exclamation marks.
- Post a first comment explaining why you built it, how it works, and what feedback you want. Readers value technical depth and honesty about limitations.
- Asking others to upvote is against the guidelines and can get a post penalized.
- Be present to answer comments for the first couple of hours.

## Reddit

- Each subreddit has its own rules in the sidebar/about page. Many limit self-promotion (a common informal guideline is that most of your activity should not be self-promotion).
- Some subreddits require flair, restrict promotion to certain days or threads, or require account age/karma.
- Posts that ask a genuine question or share a lesson usually do better than announcements.
- Never post identical text to multiple subreddits.

## Discord / Slack communities

- Most have a dedicated channel for showing projects (e.g. `#showcase`, `#projects`). Posting elsewhere is usually unwelcome.
- Keep it short: one or two sentences, a link, and a specific question.
- Do not DM members to promote a project.

## Product Hunt

- Better suited for products with a clear end-user audience and polished visuals than for libraries.
- Requires preparation: tagline, gallery images, first comment from the maker.
- Asking for upvotes directly is discouraged; asking people to check it out and leave feedback is normal.

## Dev.to / Hashnode / Medium

- Long-form articles. A tutorial or "how I built X" story that teaches something, with the project as the example, fits better than an announcement.

## Lobste.rs

- Invite-only; self-promotion is tolerated in moderation but members are expected to contribute more than they promote. Use the `show` tag for your own work.

## GitHub

- Awesome-lists: open a PR only if the project meets the list's stated criteria.
- Discussions in related projects: only if your tool directly helps with a problem being discussed, and disclose that you built it.

## Bluesky

- Tech and open-source communities are active, particularly around topic-specific custom feeds (e.g. dev feeds, language feeds).
- Self-promotion is generally well-received when framed as "here is what I built and what I learned" rather than corporate broadcast marketing.
- Custom feeds select posts by their content, hashtags, or author, so you can't post into a feed. Hashtags and the keywords feeds look for help discovery, but avoid tag stuffing; 1–3 relevant tags is plenty.
- Engaging in replies, sharing screenshots or short demo clips, and crediting upstream libraries works better than pure link drops.

## Mastodon / Fediverse

- Community norms vary widely across instances: always check the instance's local rules and code of conduct regarding self-promotion before posting.
- Clear topical hashtags (e.g. `#opensource`, `#rust`, `#python`) are how posts get found, since there's no algorithmic feed. `#introduction` is for introducing yourself, not a project. Some instances ask that promotional posts use a content warning (CW); check local rules.
- The culture strongly dislikes corporate marketing, aggressive engagement farming, uncaptioned images (always add alt text to screenshots), and unsolicited @-mentions.
- Genuine technical write-ups and open-source releases with public source code receive a much warmer reception than closed products or waitlists.
